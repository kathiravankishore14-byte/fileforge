Sooner or later something asks you for a CSV. An import screen, a database load, a mail-merge, a client's system that will not touch .xlsx. An Excel to CSV converter strips the workbook down to the thing those systems actually want: rows of values, separated by commas, in a file any tool written in the last forty years can read.

What CSV keeps, and what it cannot

CSV is deliberately minimal. Knowing the trade before you convert avoids surprises at the other end:

  • All the values. Text, numbers and dates come through as they appear in the sheet.
  • Formula results, not formulas. The computed value lands in the cell; the formula itself does not exist in CSV.
  • Proper quoting. Commas, quotation marks and line breaks inside a cell are escaped so columns do not shift.
  • One sheet only. CSV is a single table by definition — the first sheet in the workbook is the one converted.
  • No formatting. Colours, fonts, borders, column widths, merged cells and conditional formatting are all dropped.
  • No charts, images or pivot tables. Those are workbook objects, not data, and have nowhere to go in a text file.

What people convert Excel to CSV for

  • Importing into another system. CRMs, e-commerce platforms, email tools, accounting software and payroll systems almost all take CSV and many take nothing else.
  • Loading into a database. Every database has a bulk CSV loader; almost none read .xlsx directly.
  • Feeding a script. Python, R and shell tools read CSV in one line. Parsing a workbook needs a library.
  • Handing data to someone without Excel. A CSV opens on any machine, with no licence involved.
  • Version control and diffs. Two CSVs can be compared line by line; two .xlsx files are zip archives and cannot.
  • Archiving. Plain text will still be readable long after the current spreadsheet formats have moved on.

The encoding trap, and how to avoid it

The single most common complaint about any CSV export is names and accents arriving as garbled characters in Excel. The file is almost always fine — UTF-8 is the right encoding and it is what this tool writes — and the problem is that Excel on Windows guesses a legacy encoding when you double-click a .csv. The reliable route is to open Excel first, go to Data then From Text/CSV, choose the file, and set File Origin to UTF-8 in the preview dialog. Google Sheets, Numbers, LibreOffice and virtually every database read UTF-8 correctly with no extra steps.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Excel to CSV converter free?

Yes. Converting a spreadsheet to CSV here is free with no signup, no trial and no daily limit. Nothing is held back behind an upgrade, because the conversion runs on your own machine.

How do I convert Excel to CSV online?

Drop your .xlsx or .xls file onto the box on this page. The tool shows you the sheet name, how many rows it found and the first five rows, so you can check you have the right file. Press Convert and the .csv downloads.

My workbook has several sheets — what happens to them?

A CSV file holds exactly one table, so it cannot carry a whole workbook. This tool converts the first sheet in the workbook. If you need another sheet, open the workbook in Excel, move that sheet to the front or copy it into a new file, and convert that.

Do formulas come across?

You get the results, not the formulas. A cell containing =SUM(A1:A10) arrives in the CSV as the number it worked out to. That is how CSV works everywhere — the format has no concept of a formula — and it is usually what you want, since the file is normally headed for an import somewhere.

Does it accept old .xls files as well as .xlsx?

Yes, both. The modern .xlsx format and the older binary .xls from pre-2007 Excel are each read directly, with no need to open and re-save first.

What happens to formatting, colours and charts?

They are dropped. CSV is plain text — just values separated by commas — so cell colours, fonts, borders, merged cells, conditional formatting, charts and images do not survive. If you need the look preserved, <a href="/excel-to-pdf">export the sheet to PDF</a> instead.

Why do accented characters look wrong when I open the CSV in Excel?

That is Excel guessing the encoding, not a fault in the file. The .csv is written as UTF-8, and Excel on Windows often assumes something else when you double-click a CSV. Open Excel first, then use Data, then From Text/CSV, pick the file, and set File Origin to UTF-8. Google Sheets, Numbers and most databases read it correctly without any of this.

What about cells that contain commas or line breaks?

They are quoted properly. A cell holding "Smith, John" is written surrounded by quotation marks so it stays one field, and quotation marks inside a cell are escaped. Anything reading standard CSV will put the columns back exactly as they were.

Is my spreadsheet uploaded anywhere?

No. The workbook is opened and read by your own browser and nothing is sent to a server. That is the difference that matters for payroll, customer lists, patient data or anything under an NDA — there is no copy sitting on someone else's machine waiting for a deletion timer.

Is there a file size limit?

The tool does not impose one. Because the work happens on your device rather than on a server, the practical ceiling is your own browser's memory. Ordinary business spreadsheets, including ones with tens of thousands of rows, are no trouble.

Can I convert several workbooks at once?

Files are converted one at a time. Each conversion is quick, though, because there is no upload wait and no queue — you pick the next file and go.

What can open a CSV file?

Almost everything. Excel, Google Sheets, Numbers, LibreOffice Calc, any text editor, and effectively every database, CRM, accounting package and analytics tool that has an import button. That universality is the whole reason CSV is still the default handover format.

Does it work on mobile?

Yes. It runs in Chrome, Safari, Firefox and Edge on phones and tablets exactly as it does on a laptop, with nothing to install.

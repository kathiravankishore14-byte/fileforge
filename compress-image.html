Compress an image to an exact file size, free

Most image compressors give you a vague "low / medium / high" slider and leave you guessing. That's useless when a form refuses anything over 50KB. This tool works the other way round: you name the file size you need, and it finds the best quality that fits.

How to compress an image to a target size

  1. Add your image. Drag a JPG, PNG or WebP onto the box above, or tap Browse files.
  2. Pick your target size. Choose 30KB, 50KB, 100KB, 300KB or 1MB. 50KB is the default because it's the most commonly requested limit for form and ID uploads.
  3. Check the live estimate. Before you commit, the page shows the size you would actually get and how much smaller that is — so there are no surprises after the fact.
  4. Compress and download. Your file is ready in seconds.

See the result before you commit

This is the part most compressors get wrong. Elsewhere you upload the file, wait, and only then find out whether the result is usable. Here the estimate shown under the dropdown is produced by actually running the real compression search — it is the true achievable size, not a guess. Change the target and the estimate updates. You only press Compress once you already know what you're getting.

What target size should I choose?

  • Online forms and ID photo uploads — usually 50KB or 100KB. Check the form's stated maximum and pick the option at or just below it.
  • Email attachments — 300KB is comfortable and keeps several photos well under any mailbox limit.
  • Website and blog images — 100KB to 300KB. Large images are one of the most common causes of slow pages.
  • Messaging and social uploads — 300KB or 1MB, since these platforms recompress anyway.
  • Absolute smallest — 30KB, when a strict limit matters more than fine detail.

How the compression actually works

The tool searches rather than guesses. It starts at near-maximum JPEG quality and full resolution, and checks whether that already fits your target — if it does, you keep every pixel. If not, it narrows in on the highest quality that fits. Only when even the lowest usable quality is still too big does it begin reducing the image's actual dimensions, stepping down through 80%, 60%, 45%, 30% and 15% of the original size.

The order matters: quality is sacrificed before resolution, and resolution is only touched as a last resort. That's why the output tends to look noticeably better than a tool that simply crushes everything to a fixed quality setting.

What it can't do

  • It can't beat physics. A large, detailed photograph cannot become 30KB and still look good. When a target is unreachable the tool says so and gives you the smallest file it could produce, rather than pretending.
  • It won't shrink an already-small image. If your file is already under the target it's handed back untouched — recompressing would cost quality for no benefit.
  • It doesn't set exact pixel dimensions. If you need a specific width and height, use the Resize Image tool.
  • The output is always JPEG. Transparency in a PNG will be lost, since JPEG has no transparent background.

Who uses an image compressor

  • Anyone filling in an online application where the photo upload is capped at a fixed size
  • Job seekers and students attaching photos and documents to portals that reject anything larger
  • People emailing photos that bounce back as too large
  • Website owners and bloggers whose pages load slowly because of oversized images
  • Anyone sending holiday photos over a slow or metered connection

Private by design

Your image is compressed inside your own browser, on your own device. It is never uploaded to a server, never stored, and never seen by anyone else — which is exactly what you want when the photo is an ID, a passport photo or a personal document. There's no account to create and no email to hand over.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I compress an image to an exact KB size?

Drop your photo into the box above and pick a target from the list — 30KB, 50KB, 100KB, 300KB or 1MB. Before you commit to anything you'll see a live estimate of the size you'd actually get, then press Compress and download the result.

Is the image compressor free?

Yes, completely — no signup, no email, no watermark and no limit on how many images you compress. Optional premium features may be added later for advanced workflows like batch processing, but this tool's core functionality stays free.

Will compressing ruin the quality of my photo?

The tool works hard to avoid that. It searches for the highest JPEG quality that still fits inside your target size, and only reduces the pixel dimensions if quality alone can't get there. You get the best-looking file that fits, not just any file that fits.

What size should I compress my image to?

It depends where it's going. Online forms and ID photo uploads usually ask for 50KB or 100KB. Email attachments are comfortable at 300KB. For a website, aim at 100–300KB so pages stay fast. If a form states a maximum, pick the option at or just under it.

What if my image can't reach the size I picked?

You'll be told, plainly. Instead of silently handing you a blurry file, the tool reports the smallest size it could reach — for example "Best possible: 62KB" — so you can decide whether to accept it or choose a larger target.

What format is the compressed image?

A JPEG. JPEG is what makes big size reductions possible, and it's the format most forms and portals ask for. If you need PNG or WebP instead, use the Convert Image Format tool after compressing.

Are my photos uploaded to a server?

No. The compression runs entirely inside your browser using your own device's processing power. Your photo is never uploaded, which matters when it's an ID photo, a passport photo or anything personal.

Does the image compressor work on a phone?

Yes. It runs in Chrome, Safari, Firefox and Edge on Android and iPhone just as it does on a laptop, with nothing to install.
